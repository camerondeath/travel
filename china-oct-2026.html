Read on the plane

The Fourth Waterfront

You have already done the version of Shanghai that argues with itself in public. In April you stood on the Bund and turned around to look at Pudong and felt the whole conversation the city has been having with itself since 1990, the colonial facade and the state's answer to it, permanence built twice by two different regimes convinced they'd invented the idea. You don't need that argument explained to you again. What's left, on a second visit, is the part of the city that isn't performing for anyone, the water itself, which was here before the cotton port and the concessions and will be here after the second-tallest building in the world has gone the way of the Customs House, a quaint reminder of what one generation was sure would last.

Shanghai has four waterfronts and you have properly walked one of them. The Bund is the first, built to announce Western capital. Pudong is the second, built to answer it. West Bund, the reclaimed industrial shoreline south of the old city, is the third, the one where cement works and shipyards became museums without anyone pretending the transformation was anything other than real estate. This week is mostly about the fourth: Yangpu, upriver and unglamorous, the wharves and cotton mills that fed the concessions from outside their own borders, which the renovation money hasn't reached yet and may not for years. You'll be, almost certainly, the only visitor there all week.

There's a smaller argument running underneath, too. You're staying two minutes from the old walled city this time instead of down in the Concession, which changes the whole texture of what's nearby without changing what the city itself is doing. Everything below tries not to repeat April. Where it can't avoid the neighbourhood, it goes somewhere in it April didn't go.

The water comes in dirty and leaves clean. That, in the end, is the only argument on the river that has ever been telling the truth.

Chapter I · Saturday 24 October

Arrival, and the Fourth Waterfront

Land past sunrise, do almost nothing, then go and stand somewhere the city hasn't finished with yet.

You land at 6:55am on NZ289 having crossed most of a day in a flat bed, which is a genuine mercy compared to April's midnight arrival and the hour of immigration queue that followed it. There's no reason to rush this morning. Alipay or WeChat Pay bound to a foreign card at the airport before anything else, because almost nothing in this city takes a physical card and you'll be glad of it by lunch. Then DiDi into town, roughly 45 minutes to Xizang Middle Road depending on the tunnel traffic, and the first thing worth noticing is that the hotel puts you somewhere different this time: two minutes from the old walled city, five from the Bund, a wholly different centre of gravity from the Dapu Road end of the Concession you knew in April.

The room won't be ready before 3pm. Leave the bags, walk to Yu Garden's outer streets for something to eat that isn't hotel breakfast, then come back and actually rest. Jet lag deserves the afternoon. The one thing worth protecting energy for is the evening, because the fourth waterfront doesn't reward being tired.

Yangpu Binjiang is a 25 minute DiDi upriver from the hotel, past the point where the renovated Bund promenade simply stops. What's left is the working infrastructure of Shanghai's first industrial century: cotton mills, a decommissioned power station, cranes still standing over water that no longer needs them. None of it has been cleaned up for you. That's the whole point of coming. Walk the river path at dusk with no destination, and notice how different the light is here from anywhere you stood in April, industrial rather than monumental, working rather than performing.

Dinner is Sanbaibei, a short DiDi back toward the hotel, Shanghainese home cooking with a wine list that takes itself more seriously than the room does. Order more than you think you need on the first night. You have a week ahead of you and nowhere to be tomorrow before mid morning.

Chapter II · Sunday 25 October

West Bund

The third waterfront: cement works and shipyards turned into some of the most serious museums in China.

West Bund is the version of Pudong's argument made by private money instead of the state. Where Pudong says "we can build a skyline in a generation," the West Bund museums say something quieter and, in its way, more confident: we can take the actual bones of the industrial city, a cement silo, a coal handling building, and turn them into rooms good enough to hold major collections of contemporary Chinese art without needing to disguise what they used to be. It's the same move Rockbund made with a 1932 Art Deco building in April, except this time the building doesn't try to look respectable. It looks like what it was.

Tank Shanghai, five converted aviation fuel tanks from the old Longhua airport, opens at 10am and rewards arriving close to it, before the crowd. The scale inside the tanks does something similar to what the Power Station of Art did to you in April, though the geometry is stranger, circular rather than cathedral-vaulted. From there it's a short walk north along the river path to the Long Museum West Bund, a private collection built inside the shell of a former coal unloading dock, the raw concrete umbrella vaults left exposed on purpose. The collection itself is the argument: Chinese artists who came of age after 1989, hung in a building made of salvage, on a riverbank the state cleared of heavy industry to make room for exactly this. You learned to read the colonial layer in April. This is the contemporary layer, and it asks for the same kind of attention.

The evening is Monster After Dark, a different outfit and a different route from the Night Eats tour you already did in April, meeting near East Nanjing Road rather than Jiangsu Road. Three and a half hours of genuine eating rather than a tasting menu dressed up as a walk. Arrive hungry and let someone else do the ordering for one night. You'll eat differently for the rest of the week because of it.

Chapter III · Monday 26 October

M50, Properly

A full day on Suzhou Creek instead of a rushed hour on the way to somewhere else.

M50 got a glance in April, folded into a Monday of options that mostly went other places. It deserves better than a glance. Fifty Moganshan Road is a former textile mill complex along Suzhou Creek, more than a hundred galleries in buildings that still have their original saw-tooth roofs and loading bays, and the only way to actually see it is to stop treating it as a stop between other things.

ShanghART, in Building 16, has represented serious Chinese contemporary artists since 1996 and is the reason M50 exists as a district rather than a curiosity. Give it real time rather than a walkthrough. The smaller galleries around it are inconsistent by design, some closed, some between shows, some showing something genuinely worth the detour, and the only way to find out which is which is to walk the whole complex rather than pre-selecting three names off a website. This is the closest Shanghai gets to Berlin's gallery districts, and it works the same way: the good find is the one you weren't told about.

Suzhou Creek itself, running past the mill buildings, is a fifth kind of waterfront that doesn't fit neatly into the Bund/Pudong/West Bund/Yangpu framework, an inland industrial waterway rather than the Huangpu's tidal reach, and the walk along it toward the Moganshan Road bridge is worth twenty unplanned minutes on its own. Lunch somewhere in the district, nothing booked, the point of the day is that nothing needs to be. Evening back near the hotel, early, because tomorrow starts with a longer DiDi west.

Chapter IV · Tuesday 27 October

Columbia Circle

A former American country club, and the western edge of the Concession April never reached.

Columbia Circle sits on Yan'an Road West, in what was once the American Community Church's social club for the Shanghai foreign community in the 1920s and 30s, a compound of colonial villas and a distinctive Modernist water tower that has been restored into a low-rise district of design studios, restaurants and small independent shops. It's a different kind of adaptive reuse from Tank Shanghai or the Long Museum, residential and clubby rather than industrial and civic, closer in spirit to what Xintiandi was gesturing at in April before you knew enough to be skeptical of it. Columbia Circle is smaller, quieter, and less obviously commercial, and the water tower alone is worth the DiDi out.

From there the day drifts east on foot, through streets the April week's French Concession days never fully reached: Fumin Road, then south onto Yongfu Road and Anfu Road, which were mentioned in April as side streets off Wukang Road but never properly walked. These are residential lanes with a scattering of small design shops and wine bars that have opened in the last two or three years, quieter than Wukang Lu itself and largely untouched by the photographers who camp at the Wukang Mansion corner. No destination required. This is the part of the Concession that rewards slow walking rather than a sight to check off.

Dinner somewhere along Anfu Road, wherever looks busy with locals rather than a queue of visitors. Nothing booked. The day's whole argument is that this corner of the city works better without one.

Chapter V · Wednesday 28 October

The Small Galleries

Rooms that don't make the magazines, then the strangest hour in the city.

Morning belongs to the galleries that operate at a smaller scale than West Bund or M50, sunk into lane houses rather than converted factories: Capsule on Yongjia Road, BANK, and CHERUBY on Changle Road, which opened only in the last year or so. These are rooms where the work is often harder than anything on the West Bund, made to be looked at rather than collected, and if the timing lines up you may catch shows going up ahead of the November fair season, which means encountering new work with no one else in the room rather than behind a rope with a collector's assistant hovering.

The afternoon holds something odd. The Propaganda Poster Art Centre, in the basement of an ordinary residential block near the corner of Huashan and Yongjia Roads, holds several thousand posters from the decades when Shanghai was, by its own later admission, switched off: the long dormancy between the foreigners leaving in 1949 and Deng turning the lights back on in 1990. April's itinerary offered this as one option among three for a Monday morning and there's a real chance you already went. If you did, this slot is better spent as unstructured time in the same neighbourhood. If you didn't, it's worth the search, the entrance is genuinely hard to find and that's more or less the point.

Evening is deliberately open. April already covered the Concession after dark properly, Yongkang Lu, the shaokao grills, the lane houses lit from inside, and there's no reason to repeat a tour you've already done. Eat wherever the day's walking has left you, and let the last hour of the evening be whatever it wants to be.

Chapter VI · Thursday 29 October

The Other Side of the River

Shanghai Museum East, and the Pudong shoreline you've only ever looked at from a distance.

All week you've been walking waterfronts on the Puxi side, looking across at Pudong the way everyone does, as a skyline rather than a place. Today crosses over. Shanghai Museum East, in the Expo area near Houtan, opened its final galleries relatively recently and is a genuinely different building from the older People's Square branch that April's back-pocket list flagged as a rainy day option. Whether you went there in April or not, this is a separate museum with its own collection and it doesn't repeat anything. Give the bronze and jade galleries real time.

Afterward, walk the Pudong riverside path rather than DiDi-ing straight back across the tunnel. The Riverside Avenue on this bank is newer, more manicured, and considerably less crowded than the Bund promenade opposite it, which makes for an odd and genuinely useful vantage: the Bund from the water side, the colonial facade you spent a whole April morning learning to read, seen now as the thing Pudong itself is answering, rather than as the answer. This is the closing move in the four-waterfronts thread the plane essay opened with. You've now stood on all of them.

Dinner back on the Puxi side, near the hotel. Nothing elaborate required tonight. Tomorrow is deliberately empty, and it's worth being a little tired for it rather than fighting through a big evening first.

Chapter VII · Friday 30 October

Nanshi, Two Minutes From the Door

The old walled city, and a day built to have nothing in it.

Every day so far has had a reason. Today doesn't, on purpose, and it doesn't need one because of where the hotel happens to sit this time. Xizang Middle Road runs along what used to be the edge of the old walled city of Shanghai, the Chinese-administered town that existed before the concessions, encircled by a wall and moat that were demolished in 1912 and replaced by the ring road that still traces their shape. Nanshi, the old town inside that ring, is a genuinely different register from anything the Concession or the Bund offers: narrower, older in feel if not always in fact, the City God Temple at its centre still functioning as an active place of worship rather than a museum piece.

April brushed past the edge of this territory once, Laoximen mentioned in passing as an evening option and, by all appearances, not actually walked. This is the same old town, gone into properly rather than glanced at. Yu Garden's ornamental core will be full of visitors by mid morning and is worth ten minutes for the rockery alone, but the real substance of the day is in the lanes around it that the tour groups don't bother with, Fangbang Middle Road and the narrower alleys off it, where the old city's grain, low tile roofs, laundry strung between buildings, a genuine wet market a few streets over, is still legible under the restoration work.

No itinerary for the rest of it. Walk until something holds your attention, sit down somewhere with no English menu, come back to the hotel whenever the day runs out. This is the deliberate rest beat the week needs before the last big push of Hongkou tomorrow.

Chapter VIII · Saturday 31 October

Hongkou, and the Crab

Duolun Road's writers, Lu Xun's park, and the last big meal in the city.

April went into Hongkou once, briefly, on the alternate Qingming route: 1933's cattle bridges, Zhapu Lu for lunch, the Jewish Refugee Museum in the afternoon. This is the same district approached from a different corner entirely, one it never reached. Duolun Road Cultural Street, a short pedestrian lane a few blocks north, was the address of most of Shanghai's most important left-wing writers in the 1920s and 30s, Lu Xun among them in the years before he returned to live nearby, Mao Dun, Guo Moruo, a concentration of literary and political ferment in about four hundred metres of shikumen and Art Deco frontage that has been restored with unusual care rather than gutted for retail. Bronze statues of the writers sit along the lane, a small museum occupies one of the old houses, and the street is quiet enough on a Saturday morning that it's possible to actually read the plaques rather than fight a crowd for them.

Lu Xun Park, a short walk further, holds his tomb and a small dedicated museum, a genuinely different register from Shaoxing's birthplace complex in April, this is the writer in his final Shanghai years rather than his Zhejiang childhood, and the park itself, full of Shanghainese doing exactly what parks are for on a Saturday, ballroom dancing, birdcages hung in the trees, cards on stone tables, is worth sitting in for its own sake.

The evening closes the trip's eating properly. Cejerdary, on the Bund end of Guangdong Road, is an austere, almost temple-like room built around hairy crab, in season now and at its best in late October and November, the roe at its richest right in this window. Order more than feels sensible. This is the meal the whole week has been building toward without quite saying so, and there's no reason to hold back on the last serious dinner in the city.

Chapter IX · Sunday 1 November

Departure

Travel only. The morning is yours, the afternoon is the airport.

Checkout at the hotel is before noon, and the flight home doesn't leave until 2:15pm, which leaves a genuine morning rather than a rushed one. Nothing needs booking. A last walk through whichever corner of the old town or the Bund pulled hardest during the week, breakfast somewhere unremarkable and local rather than the hotel buffet on the last day, then bags and the DiDi out to Pudong.

Hongqiao isn't in play this time, the return flight leaves from Pudong, which from Xizang Middle Road is a longer run than it would have been from the Concession, budget 45 to 60 minutes depending on the tunnel and the day of the week. Business Premier check-in should be straightforward. The long flight home is the right place to actually think about what eight days in a city you already knew changed about how you saw it, rather than trying to work that out on the ground.
