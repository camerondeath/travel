Read on the plane

The Second Look

A city rewards a second visit only when you stop looking for what impressed you the first time. Shanghai in particular. In April you learned what it wants you to see. In October it will let you see what it is when nobody has come to look.

Six months ago you stood on the Bund and turned around. That gesture, so obvious as to be almost automatic, is what the city trains you to perform. The colonial facade behind you, the corkscrew of Pudong opposite, the argument between them running back and forth across the Huangpu like a rally in a tennis match neither player is prepared to concede. Everyone who visits Shanghai does this. The Chinese state, in ways it does not need to explain, wants you to. The whole city has been arranged so that the visual grammar of the encounter is difficult to avoid. It is not a small thing. It is the argument that Shanghai wanted to have with the twentieth century, and now with the twenty-first, and it is a good argument. You could spend a week looking at that river and never come to the end of what it is trying to tell you.

But you have already had that conversation. The question a second visit poses is a different one. What is left of a city once you have seen the version of itself it prepared for you, the polished sequence of Rockbund and Xintiandi and Fuxing Park and Wukang Road, the walking tours and the wet markets, the food streets that appear in every English-language guide? What remains, when the performance is over, is the room itself.

Consider the room. Not the stage, the room. There is a story Ballard tells in the memoir he wrote after Empire of the Sun. He returned to Shanghai in the 1990s, forty years after being interned as a child in Lunghua camp, and went to look at the Amherst Avenue house where his family had lived before the Japanese arrived. The building was still there, in what is now Xinhua Road in the western reach of the old French Concession. Someone had subdivided it, packed families into every room, hung laundry across the veranda where his mother had held cocktail parties. He was not distressed by this. He was, he wrote, oddly reassured. The house had gone on. The stage set of his colonial childhood had proved less durable than the walls that had held it. The lives being lived in those rooms in 1991 were closer to the real Shanghai than anything his family had ever seen, and this seemed to him not an indictment but a kind of grace.

This is the thing worth carrying with you into the October week. Shanghai is a city that has spent every one of the last hundred and eighty years being reoccupied by whatever regime happens to be in charge. British and French merchants first, then Japanese soldiers, then the Nationalists briefly, then the Communist Party for three decades of dormancy, then Deng Xiaoping's economic reforms, then the state's rediscovery of soft power and the private capital that has followed it. Each occupation has left behind buildings that it built to announce its permanence. Each has been replaced. The Sassoons expected the Cathay Hotel to be theirs forever. The Kadoories expected the Marble Hall. The People's Liberation Army marched into the International Settlement in May 1949 and reappropriated both. Deng Xiaoping declared Pudong a special economic zone in 1990 and the Chinese state, over the following three decades, built a new skyline on rice paddies and cotton fields to make a rhetorical point that had nothing to do with either. Every one of these was, in its moment, going to last forever. None of them did. What lasted was the water they were all built beside, the streets they were all built along, and the people who had been living in Shanghai before any of them arrived and were living there after each of them was gone.

The second visit is a visit to the room, not the stage. It is possible to arrange a trip to Shanghai that spends most of a week in the Concession alone, walking the same plane-tree streets you walked in April, and to call this a second look. It would not be. It would be watching the same performance from a slightly different seat. What the room offers is different. Yangpu, north of the reclaimed West Bund, where the wharves and cotton mills that fed the concessions from beyond their own borders still stand because nobody has yet had the money or the will to convert them into anything else. Nanshi, the old walled city, which the concession-era treaty maps drew a red line around so that Western law and Chinese law would not overlap. Hongkou, north of Suzhou Creek, the neighbourhood where twenty thousand Jewish refugees from Nazi Europe settled between 1933 and 1941 because Shanghai was the only city in the world that would accept them without a visa, and where, later, the writer Lu Xun spent his final years diagnosing what he saw as the moral pathology of the country he loved. These are the rooms. None of them was built for you. None of them will perform.

There is a specific pleasure available in this. Anyone who has spent time in a foreign city learns eventually that the good hours are the ones spent in places that have no obligation to notice you are there. A wet market at seven in the morning. A hutong lane where an old man is repairing a radio on the pavement. A restaurant with no English menu whose regulars have been eating there for thirty years. These are not authentic in the sense that word is usually deployed, which is to say marketable. They are simply not staged. And what a city is, underneath its stagecraft, is the sum of all the moments in which it is not being asked to prove anything to anyone.

Consider also what has changed in the six months since April. The trip you took then was structured around a specific framing, the Four Waterfronts, the argument that Shanghai's identity could be read off its treatment of the Huangpu at four distinct points. It was a good framing. It got you inside the city in a way that a more scattered itinerary would not have. But every framing is also a limit, and one of the things a second visit is for is to notice the limit. The waterfront thesis is Shanghai's own story about itself. The Bund is Shanghai's face to the West. Pudong is Shanghai's face to itself. West Bund is Shanghai's face to the future it wants. Yangpu, the fourth waterfront you did not fully walk in April, is Shanghai's face to its own working past. All of these are theses. All of them are true. And all of them position Shanghai as a city best understood in relation to what is happening at its edge, where the water meets the land. Which is to say, the city as seen from a boat.

What Shanghai looks like from inside, from a lane house courtyard or an artist's studio or someone's kitchen, is a different city. It is more ordinary and more strange. The lane house is the actual unit of Shanghai. Not the Bund building, not the Pudong tower, not the West Bund museum. The shikumen. Two storeys of grey brick, an internal courtyard, a stone arched doorway. Between the mid-nineteenth century and the mid-twentieth, most of Shanghai's population lived in some version of this. Many of them still do. Every time the international press produces one of its periodic laments about Shanghai's disappearing old neighbourhoods, this is what it is lamenting: the demolition of shikumen. The city has demolished tens of thousands of them since 1990 to make way for the towers that appear on every guidebook cover. It has also preserved some, most famously Xintiandi, which is now a shopping district in shikumen costume. What it has done less visibly is leave a great number of them alone, still occupied, still functioning, in neighbourhoods where nothing else has changed enough to make them worth photographing. The old walled city is one of these. Hongkou is another. If you want to know what Shanghai actually is, as opposed to what it wants you to think it is, you go to the shikumen the city has forgotten to demolish or restore.

A word about art. Shanghai's gallery scene has, over the past ten years, become one of the two or three most serious in Asia. This is not a projection. Auction volumes, primary sales, the fair calendar around West Bund Art & Design and Art021 in November, the international dealers who have opened Shanghai outposts, the private museums that have proliferated along the reclaimed West Bund waterfront, all of this represents a genuine consolidation of Chinese contemporary art around this city. The important thing for a visitor to understand is that the museums are not where the interesting work is. The museums are where the story of the interesting work gets told. The work itself is in the small galleries, the ones that operate from converted lane houses in the Concession, from upper floors on Anfu Road, from spaces at the top of former textile mills at M50. Capsule at 275 Anfu Road, in a 1930s garden house. BANK, now in the White Building at 457 North Shaanxi Road after two previous addresses. Antenna Space up at M50. These are galleries you should walk into by appointment, when the shows are quiet, and where, if you are alert enough to notice, you may be looking at the artists who will define the next twenty years of Chinese art before anyone outside a very small world knows their names.

And a word about eating. The instinct in a foreign city is to accumulate meals. Every night a booking. Every lunch a plan. In Shanghai this is a mistake, because the best eating available in this city is not at the restaurants that appear on curated lists. It is at the counter of a wonton shop in a residential lane at ten in the morning, at a hairy crab noodle bar on the Bund end of Guangdong Road in the third week of October when the roe is at its peak, in the kitchen of someone's home cooked by a Shanghainese ayi who has been feeding her family the same dishes for forty years. October is hairy crab season, which matters. It matters more than almost anything else on the food calendar. The male crabs mature in the tenth lunar month, the females in the ninth, and for the six weeks around the confluence of the two the best restaurants in the city serve almost nothing else. If you eat one serious meal this trip that could not be eaten at any other time, make it that. Everything else the city serves it serves every day of the year.

What you are looking for, then, in the week that follows, is not the city Shanghai wants to show you. That city you already saw. You are looking for what remains when the show is over: the rooms, the meals, the small studios, the water at Yangpu that is still a working river rather than a stage. The person who sees a city twice sees it differently the second time not because the city has changed, though of course it has, but because they know what to ignore. In April you ignored nothing. In October you can afford to. That is the whole discipline of a second look. Choose what to walk past. The city will notice you doing it, in the way that cities notice the visitors who have started to see them clearly, and it will begin, however imperceptibly, to offer you the things it did not offer the first time.

The plane will land at six fifty-five in the morning. There will be light on the tarmac. The immigration hall will be full and then, an hour later, empty. You will get in a car and be driven forty-five minutes to a hotel two minutes from the old walled city, and the driver will not ask you what you have come to see, because he assumes, correctly, that a foreigner arriving in Shanghai in late October has come to see the same things every other foreigner has come to see. What he will not know, and what you will begin to demonstrate over the eight days that follow, is that the visitor who comes back the second time is a different visitor entirely. The river is the same river. The city is the same city. The disciplines you bring to it are what has changed. Bring the discipline of the second look, and Shanghai will meet you there.

Saturday 24 October · Yangpu

Where the River Is Still Working

An early landing has, in Shanghai, a specific quality. The city at seven is already three hours into its day. The delivery scooters are on the second run. The dumpling shops on Xizang Middle Road have been open since five. By the time the taxi from Pudong clears the Yan'an tunnel and drops you at the hotel, the working city has already, invisibly, made all its most important decisions of the morning: what is being cooked for lunch, what will arrive at the wet market this afternoon, whose grandchild will be walked to school and by whom.

There is no point trying to insert yourself into any of this before nightfall. Jet lag has its own timetable and it is worth respecting. What the day is really for is the walk after dark, north of the Bund, past the point where the promenade stops being maintained and the tourists stop appearing on the map. Yangpu Binjiang was cotton mills and shipyards and a power station until the 2010s. Some of it has been converted into museums by the same municipal reclamation project that produced the West Bund. Most of it has not. The cranes are still standing over water that no longer needs them. The Yangshupu Waterworks, a red-brick pumping station built by the British in 1883 and still operating, is one of the oldest continuously functioning industrial buildings in China. Walk east from there as the light fails and the light on the far bank comes up, and you are looking at a version of the Bund view that almost nobody sees, from an angle almost nobody uses, because it involves walking somewhere the city has not yet invited you to walk. This is the room, not the stage. Begin here.

Sunday 25 October · West Bund

Concrete That Remembers What It Was

West Bund is what happens when a state government identifies eleven kilometres of decommissioned industrial waterfront and, rather than sell it to residential developers, hands it to museums. The result, over about fifteen years, is a corridor of contemporary art institutions built inside the shells of what was there before. Cement silos. Fuel tanks. Coal handling docks. Aircraft hangars. Every architect involved was told, explicitly, not to hide the original bones. This is why, walking through Tank Shanghai at ten in the morning with the door just opened and the first light on the concrete, you have the experience so specific to Shanghai's contemporary museum quarter: standing in a room whose ceiling is thirty metres above your head and whose original purpose was to hold two thousand tonnes of aviation fuel for the airport that no longer exists.

The Long Museum, ten minutes north on the same waterfront path, is the private collection of Liu Yiqian and Wang Wei, a couple who made their money in mainland Chinese finance and then spent the next twenty years assembling one of the largest private holdings of postwar Chinese art in the world. The building, by the Shanghai architect Liu Yichun, is an inverted concrete umbrella system on the site of a former coal wharf. The columns of the wharf are visible in the museum's basement. What you are looking at, when you look at the collection housed inside them, is what serious Chinese money chose to buy in the years between the Cultural Revolution's end and now, which is to say, the visual record of one country's rediscovery of its own century. The scale of the enterprise is unlike anything in the West. It is closer to what the Rockefellers or the Fricks were doing in New York in 1910. Take it seriously.

Monday 26 October · the Concession, quietly

Behind Which Door the Work Is Made

A working artist's studio is not a gallery. The distinction matters. In a gallery the work has been chosen, framed, priced and lit; you are seeing what has already been decided about it. In a studio the work is still being made, which means the room contains a version of a thing that does not yet know what it is going to be. Half-finished canvases lean against walls. Sketches are taped to the fridge. There is a kettle, a dog, a chair the artist sits in when they are not working, and the smell of whatever solvent they use. To walk into that room is to be given, briefly, the private phase of the making. Almost nobody who visits a city as a tourist ever sees it.

Shanghai's gallery scene has, over the past decade, developed a genuinely useful convention. The serious primary-market galleries, the ones that represent artists rather than resell them, will, if asked in the right way, arrange a studio visit. It is not a service they advertise. It is not on the website. But if you have written ahead, if you have taken the time to look at the roster and picked the two or three artists whose work you actually want to see more of, and if you have been to the gallery first and made yourself a person rather than a name in an inbox, the studio door will sometimes open. Capsule at 275 Anfu Road, in a 1930s garden house behind an unmarked gate, is the gallery most consistently willing to do this. Enrico Polato, who founded it in 2016, is a working curator with genuine relationships to his artists. The gallery is by appointment in any case, which means the visit is already conducted at a different register from a walk-in. Extend the conversation.

The rest of the day is what the neighbourhood offers if you stop looking for its highlights. Anfu Road on a Monday morning has a domestic quality that vanishes by Friday. The plane trees are still there, but the crowd is not, and the shops are opening slowly, and the residents are walking dogs. This is the Concession as an actual place, briefly, before it becomes a photograph again.

Tuesday 27 October · M50 and Suzhou Creek

An Inland Waterway

The Huangpu is the river Shanghai is known for. Suzhou Creek is the river Shanghai was built on. Before the Bund existed, the creek carried the cotton and silk of the Jiangsu hinterland into the port, and the tea and rice back out, and the manufactories that grew along its banks between 1860 and 1937 produced the industrial base of what became modern China. Cotton mills. Flour mills. Textile factories. The best of the buildings that housed them are still standing along a two-kilometre stretch near Moganshan Road, and at the far western end of that stretch, in an old textile mill complex, someone had the idea in 2000 to let contemporary artists rent studio space by the square metre. That someone was, in effect, wrong about the future direction of the property. M50 was supposed to be demolished. It never was.

What survived is not what the Concession galleries are. It is rougher, larger, more experimental, less commercial in an obvious sense and more commercial in a slow one. Antenna Space, on the second floor of Building 17, run by Simon Wang since 2013, represents a roster of Chinese and international artists whose work is difficult in a way that rewards patience. ShanghART in Building 16 has been there since the mill complex became galleries and is the closest thing Shanghai has to a foundational contemporary art institution: Lorenz Helbling, its Swiss founder, has been representing Chinese artists in Shanghai since 1996. The people you meet inside these two rooms know things about Chinese art that almost nobody in the West does. Ask. Be a person, not a checklist. The information you leave with will not be available in any book.

Wednesday 28 October · a table set for one

Cooked by Someone Who Knows You Are Coming

Shanghainese home cooking is a genre almost impossible to eat in a restaurant. The reason is structural. Restaurants require dishes that hold at temperature and travel from the pass to the table, which means most of the classic ben bang preparations, the slow-simmered pork belly, the drunken chicken steeped overnight, the stir-fried river shrimp done in ninety seconds and served immediately, are optimised out of the professional kitchen. The versions you get at Chun or Jesse or Fu 1015 are excellent restaurant versions. They are not what people cook at home. What people cook at home requires a home, and someone to cook it, and a small enough gathering that the timing can be controlled by one person for one table.

This has become, in the past few years, a small and unadvertised category of Shanghai eating: the private ayi table, arranged through a handful of English-speaking food operators or via a personal introduction, in which a Shanghainese cook, usually a woman in her fifties or sixties who has been feeding her own family this way for decades, will prepare a full home meal for a table of one to six people in her apartment or a rented residential kitchen. It is not cheap and it is not consistent. The good ones are rare. The best of them is a form of eating that will change your understanding of the cuisine.

The rest of the day is preparation and recovery. A morning at the Shanghai Museum East, the Pudong branch that opened in 2024, whose bronze and jade galleries are unlike anything at the People's Square branch. An afternoon in Hongkou, where the walk is what matters. The evening, at the ayi's table, is the reason you came.

Thursday 29 October · the closed door

The Building You Cannot Just Walk Into

Shanghai has an inordinate number of heritage buildings that no one is allowed to enter. The Rockefeller-funded PSA at the former Nanshi Power Station, which you saw in April, is not one of them. Nor is Rockbund. The city has been comparatively generous with its public architecture. What it has been less generous with, for reasons that are partly political and partly practical, are the residential and clubland spaces of the pre-1949 city: the Astor House Hotel where Einstein stayed and where Shanghai's first electric light was installed, the Cercle Sportif Francais, the Shanghai Race Club, the Broadway Mansions from the top of which the Japanese Army watched the city surrender in 1937. Some of these have been restored and are open, in a limited way, for public tours. Some are used by state agencies and are effectively invisible. And a few, the most interesting few, are open by private appointment to visitors who have made the arrangement in advance.

Historic Shanghai, a small heritage tour operator run by the writer Katya Knyazeva, arranges specialist walks and, on request, private access visits to buildings that are not on the standard circuit. The Astor House lobby restoration, the Ohel Rachel Synagogue at 500 North Shaanxi Road (usually closed, opens for pre-arranged groups), the interior of the Metropole Hotel. The specifics change. The point of the arrangement is that you are not on a scheduled tour with fifteen other people. You are inside a room that has not been open to visitors that week.

The alternate move, if the heritage arrangement does not come together, is Rockbund again. The building itself, the 1932 Royal Asiatic Society, is architecturally the finest surviving pre-1949 gallery interior in the city, and the shows have been consistently good since it reopened after renovation in 2010.

Friday 30 October · the walled city

Two Minutes South of the Door

Everything Shanghai calls its old city is younger than the old city. This is the small trick of Shanghai geography that becomes clear only after a few visits. The Bund is old by Chinese port standards, but it is 1840s and after, which puts it after the American Revolution. The French Concession is 1849 forward. Xintiandi's shikumen are late nineteenth century. What was Shanghai before any of these arrived, before the Opium War drew a boundary around the foreign concessions and left the Chinese city outside it, was a walled Ming-dynasty market town that had been there since the 1550s. That city is Nanshi. The wall was demolished in 1912 to make way for a ring road, which is still there. Everything inside the ring is what predates every other Shanghai you know.

Yu Garden is inside the ring. So is Fangbang Middle Road, and the still-functioning City God Temple, and a wet market that has been at the corner of Guangqi Road since Ming times. Most tourists who come to the old city come for Yu Garden and the reconstructed bazaar around it, and they leave without going three streets deeper into the neighbourhood where the actual old Shanghai still lives. If you walk south of Yuyuan Road past the temple and keep going, past the alley where children play on paved stones, past the men playing xiangqi at the small square, past the shop selling paper offerings for Qingming and funerals, you are somewhere the Concession never reached. The scale is different. The rooflines are different. The way people occupy the street is different. This is the city Ballard's Amherst Avenue villa was built to distinguish itself from. It is still here.

Saturday 31 October · the crab

Twelve Crabs to a Bowl

Hairy crab is a national obsession that operates almost entirely on the honour system of season. The Chinese mitten crab, Eriocheir sinensis, produces a specific configuration of roe and hepatopancreatic fat that reaches its peak in the female in the ninth lunar month and the male in the tenth. Outside those six weeks the crab is available but is not the crab. In the fourth week of October the male is at the top of its curve. The crabs are farmed in the freshwater of Yangcheng Lake, an hour and a half west of Shanghai in Jiangsu, and the best of them arrive at Shanghai restaurants within a day of leaving the water. What the city does with them, in the last two months of the year, is arrange most of its serious eating around them. To be here in October and not eat hairy crab is to have missed something that will not be there when you come back.

Cejerdary at 59 Guangdong Road, on the Bund end of the road, has been the most influential single restaurant in Shanghai's hairy crab culture for the past decade. The founder, Xu Jing, opened it in 2010 with a single dish: crab roe noodles, twelve crabs picked by hand into each bowl, 360 yuan a portion, the price unchanged since. The restaurant runs its own crab farms on Yangcheng Lake and processes the meat at its own facility. The room is small and austere, more Buddhist temple than Bund restaurant, with wooden furniture and no music and a menu of six items. What you have come for is one bowl of the golden crab noodles and, if you can eat it, a small dish of drunken crab, marinated raw in Shaoxing wine. In Buddhist terms this is a form of paying attention.

The morning is Hongkou, again, but the corners of it April did not reach. Lu Xun Park where he is buried. The Jewish Refugee Museum at Ohel Moishe Synagogue that documents the twenty thousand European refugees who came here between 1933 and 1941 and left in 1949. The synagogue at 62 Changyang Road is one of the two remaining in Shanghai, restored in 2007, and the small permanent exhibition upstairs is one of the more affecting historical rooms in the city. The refugees who lived in Hongkou, mostly in what became known as the "Restricted Sector" after 1943, called it Little Vienna. The bakery on the corner sold apple strudel. The cafe on Zhoushan Road served coffee. Almost none of that survives. What survives is the fact that the neighbourhood held them, without visas, without paperwork, when no other city on earth would.

Sunday 1 November · departure

An Airport Is a Kind of Sequel

The flight home departs at 2:15 in the afternoon, which is one of the most generous departure times in the international schedule. It gives back the morning. Checkout is before noon. Pudong Airport, from the old walled city, is a comfortable forty-five minutes with the Sunday traffic. There is no rush.

What the morning is really for is the last look. Everyone has their own version of this: the walk back to the place the trip started, the coffee in the neighbourhood that has, quietly, become familiar. In Shanghai the natural choice is the Bund at daybreak. Not because it is what you saw first in April but because, having spent a week not looking at it, you can now look at it clearly. From the promenade at seven in the morning the light is on the colonial facade behind you and the Pudong skyline is in shadow across the water. It is the reverse of the sunset composition you know from every guidebook. Almost nobody is there. The old men doing tai chi under the plane trees. The joggers. A fisherman on the promenade wall with a bamboo pole. The city taking its own morning at its own pace, the same way it does when nobody is looking.
